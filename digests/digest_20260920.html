ROBOTICS Paper #1

Coding Agents with an Obstacle-Aware Harness for Safe Robot Manipulation

Authors: Bingxin Xu, Yuzhang Shang, Zhen Dong, Emilio Ferrara

Venue: arXiv Preprint (2026)  |  Citations: 0  |  Read Paper  |  PDF

The Problem

A hot new idea in robotics is to let a language model — the same kind of AI that powers chatbots — write the software that controls a robot arm on the fly, with no special robot training. That works surprisingly well for getting a task done, but nobody had checked whether it's safe. Imagine a warehouse robot told to grab a box that sits right next to a fragile item or a person's hand: does it reach the box without knocking into what it's supposed to avoid?

What They Did

The researchers ran a simple test: every task paired a goal (move this object) with an obstacle the robot was explicitly told not to touch. They found the AI collided with the obstacle in most cases. Interestingly, this wasn't because the robot couldn't "see" the obstacle or didn't understand the instruction — the AI even talked about the obstacle in its own reasoning. The problem was in planning: the AI treated "finish the task" as the only thing that mattered and never made "don't hit the obstacle" a real priority. Think of a driver who knows there's a pothole, mentions it out loud, then drives straight into it because they were focused only on reaching the destination. The team's fix, called SafeHarness, is like giving that driver a route-planning app plus a rule that they must trace a clear path around hazards before moving, check it, and re-route if it's blocked. It also makes the robot pick where it grabs an object so the grab itself steers clear of the obstacle.

Key Results

  • Task success rose to 71.9% and collision avoidance to 87.5% — up 6.5 and 27.0 percentage points over the previous best method. The safety gain is the headline: cutting collisions by more than a quarter.
  • Compared to the very same AI agent running without these safeguards, SafeHarness delivered 2.3x the task success and 1.5x the safety.
  • The surprise: the failure wasn't a perception or understanding problem, which is what you'd normally suspect. The AI knew everything it needed to; it simply didn't prioritize safety when planning. That reframes where the fix has to go.

Why This Matters

As companies rush to put language-model "brains" into real robots, this is an early, concrete warning that being capable is not the same as being safe. The paper shows the gap is fixable with better planning structure rather than more training data, which is encouraging and practical for anyone deploying robots near people or delicate equipment.

Limitations & Honest Take

This was tested in a controlled setup with clearly defined obstacles represented as neat boxes; the messy real world has obstacles that move, deform, or aren't easy to outline. Even at 87.5%, roughly one in eight attempts still collides — nowhere near safe enough for a robot working next to a person. It's a strong research step, not a shippable safety guarantee.

Bottom Line

AI-controlled robots know how to avoid hazards but don't bother to unless you force them to plan around them first — and this paper shows how to force that.

AI/ML Paper #2

Embedding Models Measure in Peculiar Ways

Authors: Juri Opitz, Andrianos Michail

Venue: arXiv Preprint (2026)  |  Citations: 0  |  Read Paper  |  PDF

The Problem

Modern AI turns words and phrases into long lists of numbers called "embeddings," which let a computer judge how similar two things are — that's what powers search engines, recommendation systems, and chatbots retrieving facts. But do these systems actually understand plain, objective quantities like "2 kilograms" versus "2000 grams" (which are exactly equal)? If they don't, an AI could badly misjudge anything involving weights, distances, times, or volumes — think of a shopping assistant that treats "500 ml" and "0.5 liters" as unrelated, or a document search that fails to connect "30 minutes" with "half an hour."

What They Did

The researchers picked measurements precisely because there's no ambiguity: unlike "happy" versus "joyful," where similarity is fuzzy, "1 mile" and "1.6 kilometers" are objectively the same distance. So they could check the AI's number sense against ground truth. They fed embedding models a range of physical measurements and asked, in effect, "does the AI's internal map put equal quantities close together and different ones far apart, in the right proportions?" It's like testing whether someone who claims to understand money treats a dollar and four quarters as equal — a basic sanity check that should be easy to pass.

Key Results

  • The models only weakly captured real physical measurement — their internal sense of "how far apart" two quantities are often didn't match the actual numeric gap.
  • Instead, similarity was driven heavily by superficial spelling overlap: two measurements that merely looked alike as text were treated as similar, regardless of whether they were actually close in value. So "1000 m" and "1 km" might land far apart because the characters differ, even though they're identical distances.
  • Recalibration didn't rescue it. The team tried adjusting how similarity is computed, and it barely helped — suggesting the blind spot is baked deeper into how these models represent text.

Why This Matters

A great deal of AI infrastructure quietly assumes embeddings "get" the meaning of the text they process. This shows a specific, common category — numbers with units — where that assumption breaks. Anyone building AI for science, engineering, finance, health, or e-commerce, where precise quantities matter, should be aware the model may be matching on appearance rather than actual magnitude. A practical upshot: if your system needs to reason about quantities, it may be safer to parse the numbers out explicitly and compare them with ordinary arithmetic rather than trusting the AI's fuzzy sense of similarity.

Limitations & Honest Take

The study diagnoses the problem clearly but doesn't hand over a fix — their attempted correction didn't work well, so the solution is still open. It also focuses on physical units specifically; the paper doesn't claim every kind of numeric reasoning fails the same way, and it doesn't test whether the largest, newest models close the gap. Treat it as a well-aimed warning to verify, not as a verdict that embeddings are useless.

Bottom Line

AI's sense of similarity often judges measurements by how they're spelled, not what they actually weigh or measure — so don't trust it with numbers-plus-units without checking.

ROBOTICS Paper #3

Workspace Models: Lightweight Robotic Memory via Saliency-Driven Supervision

Authors: Nitish Dashora, Douglas Chen, Idan Shenfeld, John Marangola, Pulkit Agrawal

Venue: arXiv Preprint (2026)  |  Citations: 0  |  Read Paper  |  PDF

The Problem

Many useful robot tasks require memory: to put away groceries, a robot needs to remember which cupboard it already filled; to finish assembling something, it must recall what steps it did. But giving a robot its entire history to reason over is both slow and error-prone — it gets distracted by irrelevant details, like a person who can't focus because they're replaying every second of their day. So robots need to remember the right things cheaply.

What They Did

The common fix is to have a big, powerful vision-language AI (the kind that can look at a scene and describe what matters) constantly summarize what's important — but running that heavy AI at every moment while the robot works is expensive and sluggish. The researchers' trick is to move that expensive thinking to training time instead of doing time. During training, the powerful AI studies the task and points out which past and present details actually matter; the system then compresses that judgment into a small, fast memory shortcut they call a "workspace token." At deployment, the robot just consults this compact memory instead of calling the expensive AI over and over. Think of a chef who, while learning a recipe, works out exactly which ingredients and steps matter, then writes a single tidy index card — and on a busy night just glances at the card instead of re-reading the whole cookbook.

Key Results

  • The lightweight memory works as a drop-in replacement for feeding the robot full observations, and it succeeded on memory-heavy tasks in both simulation and on real hardware without any expensive AI running during the task.
  • The surprise: the compact memory didn't just run faster — it actually improved the robot's performance. Cutting out the clutter helped the robot make better decisions, not just cheaper ones.
  • By skipping heavy AI queries at run time, it removes a major source of lag, which matters for robots that must act in real time.

Why This Matters

Cost and speed are two of the biggest barriers to putting capable robots into homes, warehouses, and factories. Showing that a robot can have a useful long-term memory without an expensive AI narrating every moment makes memory-dependent tasks far more practical to deploy at scale, and the bonus accuracy gain is a rare "cheaper and better" result.

Limitations & Honest Take

Because the important-information judgments are baked in during training, the approach may struggle with situations the training never anticipated — a robot could forget to remember something its designers didn't foresee as relevant, and re-teaching it means retraining rather than a quick tweak. It also still leans on a powerful vision-language AI during training, so it lowers the cost of running the robot, not the cost of building it. The tests, while including real hardware, are still research-scale; robustness across the messy variety of real homes and workplaces remains to be proven.

Bottom Line

Robots can be taught, during training, to remember only what matters — giving them cheap, fast memory that actually works better than remembering everything.

AI/ML Paper #4

Can 4D Foundation Models Remember?

Authors: Guangzhao He, Hadar Averbuch-Elor, Wei-Chiu Ma

Venue: arXiv Preprint (2026)  |  Citations: 0  |  Read Paper  |  PDF

The Problem

For a robot, a self-driving car, or an AR headset to act sensibly, it has to remember the world even when it can't currently see it — if a pedestrian steps behind a truck, the system should know they still exist and are still moving. The newest AI models can reconstruct moving 3D scenes from video ("4D" means 3D plus time), but nobody had rigorously tested whether they actually remember things once those things leave the frame, versus just forgetting them.

What They Did

The hard part of testing memory is that once an object goes off-screen, there's usually no reliable record of where it went to compare against. The researchers' clever fix is to use 360-degree videos as an all-seeing reference: because the camera captures everything around it, they always know the true position and appearance of an object, even when a normal narrow-view model would have lost sight of it. Using this, they built a test suite called PersistBench that scores AI on three human-like memory skills: knowing an object still exists after it disappears (object permanence — the thing a baby learns with peekaboo), keeping its motion smooth and believable while unseen, and remembering what it looked like. It's like quizzing someone on what happened behind their back, when you had a hidden camera the whole time.

Key Results

  • Across many current models, memory was only short-term — they stayed consistent for a moment but degraded sharply once an object left the field of view.
  • The blunt takeaway the authors coin: "seeing is not remembering." These models are good at perceiving what's in front of them and poor at holding onto it.
  • Existing benchmarks missed this because they judged results pixel-by-pixel and had no ground truth for vanished objects; PersistBench is the first to measure memory in an object-by-object way with a reliable reference.

Why This Matters

Reliable memory of unseen objects is a safety-critical capability for self-driving cars and robots — misjudging a temporarily hidden pedestrian or obstacle can be dangerous. By giving the field a clear, honest yardstick and naming exactly where today's models fall short, this work steers future development toward the memory problem rather than letting it hide behind flattering metrics.

Limitations & Honest Take

This paper diagnoses the weakness and provides the measuring stick, but it doesn't fix the underlying models — that's left to future work. And the test relies on 360-degree footage, which is a smart proxy but still a curated setting rather than the full chaos of real streets and homes; it measures whether a model remembers an object, not whether a downstream car or robot would then act safely on that memory. It's a valuable reality check, not a solution.

Bottom Line

Today's cutting-edge scene-understanding AIs see the world well but forget things the moment they go out of view — and now there's finally a proper test to prove and track it.

AI/ML Paper #5

SplashSplat: Reconstructing Splashing Liquids from Real-World Multi-View Videos

Authors: Peiyu Liu, Dingxi Zhang, Federico Tombari, Marc Pollefeys, Christina Tsalicoglou

Venue: arXiv Preprint (2026)  |  Citations: 0  |  Read Paper  |  PDF

The Problem

Splashing water is one of the hardest things to capture and recreate digitally: a splash lasts a fraction of a second, breaks into sheets and droplets, is nearly see-through and textureless, and looks completely different from every angle. That's why film effects, product design (think spill-proof packaging), and safety or engineering simulations of liquids are so painstaking. Until now there wasn't even a good real-world dataset of splashes to work from, so research leaned on smoke or fake, computer-generated liquids.

What They Did

The team first built the missing raw material: they filmed 20 real scenes — from smooth pouring streams to violent splashes — using seven synchronized 4K cameras at 60 frames per second, and carefully marked out the liquid in every frame. Then they built a reconstruction method, SplashSplat, on one sensible principle: only impose physics where the cameras can actually confirm it, rather than guessing. In plain terms, it figures out the liquid's shape from the marked outlines, estimates how that shape is flowing from one instant to the next, and then places tiny trackable "carriers" that ride along with the flow, get corrected against each new camera view, and are re-scattered wherever the system loses track. It's like reconstructing a fireworks burst by following individual sparks and constantly checking them against your photos, instead of inventing a full physics simulation from scratch.

Key Results

  • SplashSplat beat the state-of-the-art dynamic-scene reconstruction methods on both the new real-world captures and a synthetic benchmark.
  • Its results showed more physically believable motion — the water moves the way water actually moves — and it did so at lower training (computing) cost than competitors.
  • A bonus: the same reconstruction can be slowed down (temporal interpolation) or restyled without redoing the expensive work, so one capture yields many uses.
  • The dataset itself is arguably as valuable as the method: it's described as the first synchronized multi-view dataset of real splashing liquids, giving the whole field something to build on.

Why This Matters

Realistic liquid capture has direct payoffs in film and game visual effects, in advertising, and potentially in engineering and safety analysis where you'd like to study real spills and splashes rather than approximations. By cracking one of the genuinely hard cases in 3D reconstruction and releasing a benchmark, this work opens a door that was effectively closed.

Limitations & Honest Take

The method depends on an elaborate rig — seven calibrated 4K cameras and manually refined per-frame masks — so it's a lab capability today, not something you'll do with a phone. It's tuned to the specific challenge of splashing liquids, and how well it extends to other tricky see-through or fast phenomena is unproven. Impressive proof of concept; not yet a push-button tool.

Bottom Line

Researchers built the first real-world splash dataset and an AI that reconstructs splashing water more realistically and cheaply than anything before — a lab-grade breakthrough for realistic liquid effects.
