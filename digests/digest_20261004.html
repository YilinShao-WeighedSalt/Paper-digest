AI/ML Paper #1

Moore, Escher, Penrose: A Conformal Golden Braid

Authors: Sophia Feldman, Assaf Shocher

Venue: arXiv Preprint (2026)  |  Citations: 0  |  Read Paper  |  PDF

The Problem

The artist M.C. Escher is famous for impossible, self-referential pictures — his "Print Gallery" shows a man in a gallery looking at a print that contains the very gallery he is standing in. These kinds of images are mesmerizing but notoriously hard to make, because the picture has to fold back into itself with mathematical precision. Today's AI image generators are great at producing pretty pictures from a text prompt, but they have no idea how to honor that kind of strict "the image must contain itself" rule.

What They Did

Think of a modern AI image generator as an artist who starts with visual static and gradually sharpens it into a finished picture, one pass at a time. If you simply ask it (via a text prompt) for a self-referential scene, it ignores the recursion. If you let it finish a normal picture and then warp it into the spiral shape afterward, the seams don't line up and the illusion breaks. The authors' trick is to weave the warping into the drawing process rather than bolting it on at the end. They alternate — "braid" — between two viewpoints: steps that develop the plain, un-warped scene, and steps that work in the warped, spiral geometry to make the edges connect seamlessly. To make the warp reversible (it normally isn't — you can't un-blend a smoothie), they built a careful mathematical "best-guess undo" operation. The result is that the scene and its distortion grow up together, so the final image is self-consistent by construction.

Key Results

  • They successfully generated new "Print Gallery"-style images where the picture recursively contains itself, something prompting alone cannot do.
  • They showed that applying the warp only at the end, or only as a filter during generation, both fail — the AI either "repairs" the intended distortion or drifts off the required geometry.
  • Their braided approach keeps the image inside the realm of realistic-looking pictures while still obeying the strict recursive geometry, and they extended it to other self-referential transformations beyond Escher's original.

Why This Matters

This is a neat demonstration that you can impose hard mathematical or physical constraints on AI image generators without retraining them — you steer the generation process itself. That idea generalizes well beyond art: anywhere you need generated images to obey a rule the AI wasn't explicitly taught (symmetry, perspective, tiling patterns), this "interleave the constraint into the process" recipe is a useful template.

Limitations & Honest Take

This is a niche, art-focused proof of concept, not a product — it handles a specific family of geometric transformations and still requires custom mathematical setup for each new effect. It is also computationally fiddly and there is no evidence yet it scales to arbitrary real-world constraints. Treat it as an elegant research curiosity that hints at a broader technique, not something your team will deploy next quarter.

Bottom Line

Researchers taught an AI to draw Escher-style pictures that contain themselves by weaving the geometric trick into the drawing process instead of applying it afterward.

AI/ML Paper #2

Sphere Encoder 2

Authors: Kaiyu Yue, Sean McLeish, Ruchit Rawal, Brian Bartoldson, Menglin Jia

Venue: arXiv Preprint (2026)  |  Citations: 0  |  Read Paper  |  PDF

The Problem

AI systems that generate images are often slow and expensive, because the good ones refine an image over many steps. There is a faster family of generators that can produce an image in a single step, but they tend to come out blurry or low quality. The real-world stakes are cost and speed: if you can make a crisp image in one shot instead of fifty, you cut the computing bill and the waiting time dramatically — which matters for anything from design tools to on-device apps.

What They Did

Imagine every possible image as a point on the surface of a giant globe (a "sphere" in many dimensions). To create a new image, this system picks a random point on the globe and translates it into a picture. The authors found two bugs in how the original version of this system worked. First, when you pick random points, they tend to cluster around the globe's "equator," but the training process only ever practiced near the "poles" — so the system was being tested in a region it had never rehearsed, like a pianist who only practiced the low keys being asked to play the high ones. Second, the way it was trained graded it on matching images pixel-by-pixel, which quietly rewards playing it safe and producing a blurry average of all plausible answers rather than one sharp, committed image. Sphere Encoder 2 fixes both: it trains across the whole globe including the crowded equator, and it changes the grading so the system is pushed to produce sharp detail instead of hedging.

Key Results

  • Substantially better image quality than the original Sphere Encoder, while keeping the key selling points: one-step (fast) generation and a simple design.
  • The fix addressed a real mismatch — the gap between where the system was trained and where it was actually used at generation time.
  • Sharper, more detailed images by moving away from the "average everything" training signal that caused blur.
  • The models are open-sourced on GitHub, so others can verify and build on them.

Why This Matters

Fast, single-step image generators are attractive precisely because they are cheap and simple, but quality has held them back. Closing that quality gap without sacrificing speed makes this class of model more competitive for practical use, and the two problems they diagnosed (training/usage mismatch, blur-inducing loss) are common pitfalls that other researchers can now watch for.

Limitations & Honest Take

This is an incremental improvement to one specific approach, not a new paradigm, and the abstract doesn't claim it beats the best slow, multi-step generators on raw quality — the pitch is "much better than before, while staying fast and simple." How it stacks up against the broader state of the art, and on which kinds of images, would need a careful read of the full results.

Bottom Line

A fast one-shot image generator got meaningfully sharper by fixing a training blind spot and a blur-inducing shortcut, without giving up its speed.

EMBEDDED SYSTEMS Paper #3

One Basis to Animate Them All: Gaussian Blendshape Distillation for Real-Time Avatars

Authors: Ramazan Fazylov, Stamatis Lefkimmiatis, Ivan Laptev

Venue: arXiv Preprint (2026)  |  Citations: 0  |  Read Paper  |  PDF

The Problem

Realistic 3D avatars — the kind you'd use in video calls, games, or VR — can now be drawn very fast, but animating them (making the face smile or the body move naturally) is still slow because it leans on heavy AI computation for every single frame. That bottleneck is exactly what stops lifelike avatars from running smoothly on ordinary phones. If you want this to work in a real app on a normal device, you have to make the animation cheap enough to run 30-60 times per second.

What They Did

The key insight is borrowed from how animators have worked for decades: most facial expressions and body poses can be built by mixing together a small set of standard building-block poses, called "blendshapes" — a bit of "raised eyebrow" plus a bit of "open mouth" plus a bit of "turned head," dialed up or down like sliders on a mixing board. The authors discovered that the expensive AI avatars are secretly doing something very close to this simple slider-mixing under the hood. So instead of running the big AI model every frame, their method (called GALA) learns a tiny, lightweight predictor that just outputs the slider settings, and then blends the pre-computed building blocks together — a cheap arithmetic operation. They also compress the set of building blocks smartly so it fits in a tight memory budget. Crucially, this bolts onto existing avatar models without retraining them from scratch.

Key Results

  • Cut the processing cost of animation by up to a thousandfold (three orders of magnitude) on the CPU, while keeping most of the visual quality.
  • Reached up to 60 frames per second on mobile devices — smooth, real-time animation on a phone.
  • Worked across three different avatar systems (facial expressions and full bodies with moving clothing) without changing the original models.
  • Generalized to new people's faces it hadn't seen during training — a strong sign the "slider-mixing" structure is genuinely shared, not memorized.

Why This Matters

This is the kind of practical win that moves a flashy research demo onto real hardware. Making lifelike avatars run in real time on phones opens doors for telepresence, virtual try-on, games, and VR/AR without needing a powerful server in the loop. The deeper finding — that fancy learned avatar models hide a simple linear structure — may let others dramatically speed up related systems too.

Limitations & Honest Take

The approach preserves "most" of the rendering quality, not all — there is some fidelity trade-off, and very extreme or unusual expressions that don't decompose neatly into the standard building blocks may suffer. It also depends on having a good pretrained avatar model to distill from in the first place; it speeds up existing systems rather than creating avatars itself.

Bottom Line

By realizing fancy 3D avatars are basically mixing a few standard poses, researchers made them animate up to 1,000x cheaper and run at 60fps on a phone.

AI/ML Paper #4

KaliBench: A Fine-Grained Benchmark for Cybersecurity Tool Use on Kali Linux with Runtime-Free Verifiable Rewards

Authors: Pengfei Li, Naufal Suryanto, Sicheng Zhang, Muzammal Naseer

Venue: arXiv Preprint (2026)  |  Citations: 0  |  Read Paper  |  PDF

The Problem

Companies increasingly want AI assistants to help security analysts do their jobs — for example, turning a plain-English request like "scan this network for open ports" into the exact command a security tool needs. The catch is that these tools are brutally unforgiving: one wrong flag, a mistyped option, or arguments in the wrong order, and the command either fails or does the wrong thing entirely. So the real question is not "does the AI know about security?" but "can it produce the precise, runnable command every time?" — and nobody had a rigorous way to measure that.

What They Did

The authors built KaliBench, essentially a giant, carefully-graded exam for AI models. It contains 8,504 matched pairs of "here's what a human wants" and "here's the exact correct command," covering 1,642 real security tools. The hard part of such an exam is grading: there are often several valid ways to write the same command. So they built an answer key that understands aliases and equivalent phrasings, and they verified the correct answers through a multi-step pipeline — AI checking, actually running commands in a sandboxed (safely isolated) terminal, and human review. A clever bonus: because the grading is automatic and reliable, the same exam can be used as a training signal to teach smaller models to get better, without needing to spin up and run live systems every time.

Key Results

  • No open model scored above 42% on getting the exact command right when it wasn't given hints about which tool to use — a blunt reminder that current AI is far from reliable at this.
  • The benchmark is fine-grained: it separately measures whether the model picks the right tool and whether it constructs the arguments correctly, across 23 skill areas and 5 phases of security work.
  • Using KaliBench as a training signal, they boosted a small 8-billion-parameter model to rival a model roughly 85x larger (685 billion parameters) — a big efficiency win.

Why This Matters

This gives the field an honest yardstick for a genuinely useful and risky capability. The headline 42% number is a healthy dose of reality: anyone rushing to put AI in charge of security tooling should know it still makes command-level mistakes most of the time. And the finding that a small, cheap model can be trained up to near-giant performance on this task points toward affordable, specialized security assistants.

Limitations & Honest Take

Scoring the exact command is strict — a model might propose a reasonable, working alternative that the test still marks wrong, so real-world usefulness could be somewhat higher than 42% suggests (and the benchmark measures command-writing, not whether using the tool was the right call). There's also an inherent dual-use tension: a tool that teaches AI to wield security tools more precisely helps defenders and attackers alike, so it needs responsible handling.

Bottom Line

A rigorous new exam shows today's AI gets security commands exactly right less than half the time — but can be cheaply trained to do far better.

ROBOTICS Paper #5

Reconstruct, Practice, Go Real: Guided Self-Improvement for Embodied Agents

Authors: Yen-Jen Wang, Haozhe Jiang, Shuying Deng, Haoru Xue, Weirui Ye

Venue: arXiv Preprint (2026)  |  Citations: 0  |  Read Paper  |  PDF

The Problem

Teaching robots to reliably do real-world manipulation tasks — picking things up, opening drawers, handling objects — usually demands enormous ongoing human effort: engineers hand-crafting skills, designing reward signals, and tuning how the robot sees and acts. That human bottleneck is the main reason capable robots are so expensive and slow to deploy. The dream is a robot that gets better largely on its own, the way a person practices a skill until they've got it.

What They Did

The authors built a system called RPG — Reconstruct, Practice, Go Real — that lets a robot improve itself without rewriting its underlying AI brain. The clever part: instead of practicing in the physical world (slow, risky, expensive), the robot rebuilds the relevant tasks inside a simulator — a video-game-like sandbox — and practices there at high speed. When it fails, it acts like a coach reviewing game tape: it uses behind-the-scenes information the simulator can provide, plus recordings of the task, to diagnose why it failed. Then it writes itself new reusable "plays" (symbolic skills), fixes old ones, and even rewrites its own instruction sheet. Before keeping any change, it tests whether that change actually helps across different tasks, so it doesn't learn bad habits. Finally, a capable AI model uses the polished instruction sheet and skill library to run the real robot — "go real."

Key Results

  • Task success jumped from 28.6% after the first practice round to 95.0% after 15 rounds of self-improvement — roughly going from "fails two out of three times" to "nearly always works."
  • Beat all the systems it was compared against, including strong baselines (ASPIRE at 75.5% and a GPT-6-powered agent at 60.0%).
  • The improved system, once frozen, succeeded in all 30 real-world physical trials across three tasks — showing the gains learned in simulation carried over to actual hardware.
  • Achieved all this *without* updating the AI model's internal weights — improvement came from better skills and instructions, not expensive retraining.

Why This Matters

The expensive, human-intensive part of robotics is exactly the skill-building and debugging this system automates. If a robot can rehearse in simulation, diagnose its own mistakes, and reliably transfer the results to the real world, it dramatically lowers the cost and labor of getting robots to work — a potential step toward robots that adapt to new tasks on their own rather than needing a team of engineers for each one.

Limitations & Honest Take

The whole approach hinges on being able to rebuild tasks accurately in simulation, and the hardest, messiest real-world situations (deformable objects, unpredictable environments) are precisely the ones simulators struggle to reproduce faithfully — so the impressive sim-to-real transfer here was shown on a controlled set of 22 tasks, not open-ended chaos. It also leans on a powerful AI model and good starting data; it refines a capable system rather than building competence from nothing.

Bottom Line

A robot taught itself to go from failing most of the time to a 95% success rate — and nailed every real-world trial — by rehearsing and debugging itself in simulation instead of needing constant human engineering.
